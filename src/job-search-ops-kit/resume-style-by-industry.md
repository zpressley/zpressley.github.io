# Resume Trends & Finding the Right Style for the Industry

The tailoring skill in this kit defaults to a tech/ops-style resume:
single-column, ATS-safe, keyword-dense, one clean font. That's the right
default for tech, startups, and most corporate ops/business roles as of
2026, but it's the wrong style for several other fields. This doc covers
what's actually current, plus a prompt for figuring out the right style
before defaulting to the tech template.

## What's current across most industries (2026)

- **Single-column layout is back in favor.** After a few years of
  stylish two-column templates, single-column is now the safer default,
  because it parses more reliably across ATS platforms. If a template uses
  columns, sidebars, text boxes, or graphics, that's a real risk, not a
  style choice.
- **Reverse-chronological remains the dominant format.** A hybrid
  format (skills section up top, chronological history below) is a good
  alternative specifically for tech and for people with a broad skill set
  that doesn't map cleanly to one job-title progression.
- **Skills-based ATS filtering is the single biggest scoring factor right
  now.** A clearly labeled, well-organized skills section (grouped into
  something like Technical Skills / Tools / Certifications / Languages)
  is weighted heavily, more heavily than the same skill mentioned once
  inside a bullet.
- **Quantified bullets matter more than ever.** Aim for the strong
  majority of bullets (roughly 70%+) to include a real number: percent,
  dollar amount, headcount, time saved, multiple. A bullet describing a
  duty instead of a result now scores measurably worse.
- **Modern ATS use semantic matching, not just exact keyword matching.**
  A posting that says "agile development" will also credit "scrum,"
  "sprint planning," "iterative delivery." Use the real vocabulary of the
  field, not just a copy of the exact words in the posting.
- **Hidden/white text keyword stuffing is now actively detected and
  penalized.** Workday, Greenhouse, and Lever specifically flag this,
  it can trigger an auto-rejection with a fraud flag on the candidate
  record, not just get ignored. Never do this, even as a "hack."
- **No generic objective statements.** A resume summary (2-3 sentences on
  who you are and the value you bring) has fully replaced the old
  "seeking a challenging position" objective line.
- **Length:** one page remains the default for early-career or lateral
  moves; two pages is acceptable with 10+ years of relevant depth or
  real leadership scope, not just to fit more bullets in.
- **Format for submission:** keep both a clean .docx and a text-based PDF
  (never an image-only/scanned-look PDF) and check whichever gets
  submitted through an actual resume parser/checker before sending, since
  parsing quality still varies by platform.

## Where the tech-style default breaks down

Don't apply the above blindly. Several fields have meaningfully
different conventions:

- **Academia / research:** uses a CV, not a resume. Multi-page, includes
  a full publications list, grants, conference presentations, teaching
  experience. Length caps don't apply the same way.
- **Government / federal (e.g., USAJobs in the US):** resumes are much
  longer and more detailed than private-sector norms, often 3-5+ pages,
  with specific formatting and content requirements unique to the
  platform. Don't compress a federal resume down to a tech-style one-pager.
- **Legal:** conservative, traditional, no design elements at all. It sits
  closer to the pre-2020 tech resume norm than the current tech trend.
- **Finance / banking / management consulting:** still fairly
  conservative and traditional; pedigree signals (school, prior firm
  names) carry more relative weight than in tech; less tolerance for
  stylistic flourish.
- **Creative / design fields:** a portfolio matters more than the resume
  itself in many cases; some employers do want to see design sensibility
  in the resume's own layout, but a plain ATS-safe version should still
  exist for anything submitted through an online application system.
- **Healthcare / clinical:** licensure and certifications need to be
  prominent and exactly correctly named; conservative formatting is the
  norm; chronological is standard.
- **Nonprofit / mission-driven orgs:** still wants quantified impact, but
  framed around mission/community outcomes rather than pure revenue
  metrics. The "what/for whom/to what extent" bullet structure still
  works, just weighted toward impact-on-people rather than dollars.

## The prompt: figuring out the right style before defaulting to tech

Use this before generating a resume for someone whose industry isn't
confirmed tech/startup/general-corporate:

> Before building this resume, confirm the target industry and company
> type rather than assuming a tech-style format. Ask directly if it's
> not already clear from context: What industry/field is this for?
> Is the target company more traditional/conservative or more modern/
> startup-flavored? Is this an academic, government, legal, or creative
> role specifically (these have meaningfully different conventions than
> a standard corporate resume)? Based on the answer, pick the
> appropriate convention set from the list above rather than defaulting
> to single-column ATS-tech style, and say explicitly which convention
> set you're using and why, so the person can correct you if you picked
> wrong.

The underlying accuracy rules (never invent, quantify what's real, flag
gaps) apply regardless of which style convention is chosen. Only the
formatting and tone conventions change by industry, not the standard for
truthfulness.
