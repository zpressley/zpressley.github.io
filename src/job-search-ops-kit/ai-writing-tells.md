# What Makes Writing Sound AI-Generated (and How to Avoid It)

Recruiters, hiring managers, and increasingly the ATS systems themselves
are getting fast at spotting AI-written resumes and cover letters — and
"this was obviously written by ChatGPT" is now an active red flag in
hiring, not a neutral non-issue. This is a reference on the actual
patterns that give it away, so anything generated through this system can
be checked against a real list instead of a vague feeling.

## The tells

**Em-dash overuse.** The single most common tell. Human writers use em
dashes occasionally; AI output tends to lean on them constantly as a
default way to add a clause. If a paragraph has more than one, it usually
reads as generated. Use periods, commas, or just restructure the sentence.

**The rule-of-three reflex.** "Not just X, but Y and Z." "Fast, reliable,
and scalable." AI defaults to triplets constantly, even when the idea only
needed one or two items. Real writing has uneven, asymmetric lists.

**Throat-clearing openers.** "In today's fast-paced business
environment," "In the ever-evolving landscape of," "As a highly motivated
professional." These add zero information and are almost pure AI tell.
Cut the sentence and start with the actual point.

**Buzzword clustering without specificity.** "Leverage synergies to drive
impact," "spearhead cross-functional initiatives to optimize outcomes."
Sounds impressive, says nothing concrete. The fix is always the same:
replace the abstract verb with the actual thing that happened, and add a
real number.

**Perfectly symmetric bullets.** If every bullet in a section is almost
exactly the same length and follows an identical grammatical structure, it
reads as generated. Real accomplishments vary in scope and are usually
better described with some sentences short and punchy, others longer.

**Repeated sentence/bullet openers.** Starting every bullet in a section
with the same word ("Led," "Managed," "Developed," repeated down the
list) is a giveaway. Vary the first word of every bullet in the same
section — mechanically check this rather than trusting your ear.

**Philosophy-statement closers.** Cover letters that end with something
like "I believe that true innovation comes from the intersection of
passion and precision" instead of an actual ask ("I'd welcome the chance
to talk about this role") read as generated filler dressed up as
sincerity.

**Excessive hedge-qualifier stacking.** "Truly remarkable and genuinely
innovative," "incredibly passionate and deeply committed." Real writing
usually picks one adjective, not two reinforcing each other.

**Generic transition words.** "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally,"
"It's worth noting that" — used to glue ideas together instead of
actually connecting them logically. Cut these and check if the sentences
still flow; usually they do, better.

**Over-balanced "on the other hand" framing.** Presenting every point with
an artificially equal counterpoint, even when the actual situation isn't
balanced. Real assessments are lopsided when the evidence is lopsided.

**Vague superlatives without evidence.** "A proven track record of
success," "extensive experience driving results" — with no specific
example or number attached in the same sentence. If a claim can't be
followed by a concrete fact, cut the claim.

## How to actually check for these

Don't just try to "write more naturally" and hope — check mechanically:
- Count em dashes in the document. More than one or two total, revise.
- Read down the first word of every bullet in a section. Any repeats, fix
  them.
- Read the cover letter's closing sentence alone, out of context. If it
  sounds like a mission statement instead of a specific ask, rewrite it.
- Search for the buzzword list above (leverage, synergy, spearhead,
  seamless, robust, cutting-edge, game-changing, elevate) and replace each
  instance with the concrete thing it's standing in for.
- Read bullets in a section back to back. If they all sound
  interchangeable with the company name swapped out, they're too generic
  — they should sound like they could only be about this specific person's
  specific accomplishment.

## Why this matters beyond just "sounding better"

This isn't purely aesthetic. Recruiters increasingly screen for AI-
generated-sounding language as a proxy for "did this person actually put
effort into tailoring this," and some are explicit that a resume reading
as generic/AI-written gets deprioritized regardless of the underlying
qualifications. The fix isn't avoiding AI assistance — it's making sure
what comes out the other end reads like it was written by someone who
actually did the things it describes, in their own voice.
