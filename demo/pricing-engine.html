Tiers, and why the overage is capped

Volume above a tier's ceiling bills at an overage rate, but capped at every step against the next tier's flat price. Without the cap, sitting just above a boundary could cost more than moving up a tier, which is the kind of thing a customer notices on an invoice.

The part worth explaining

Price is a function of cost: price = cost / (1 − margin). But one cost line is a percentage of the final price, so cost is also a function of price. Neither resolves without the other already being known.

Google Sheets has an iterative-calculation setting for exactly this, and the original build deliberately didn't use it: it's a file-level toggle, and it fails silently if a collaborator's copy has it switched off. A pricing model that quietly returns wrong numbers depending on someone else's settings is worse than one that doesn't run. So the loop was unrolled by hand into about twenty sequential blocks: compute cost from the prior price, compute price from that cost, repeat. That converges in a handful of rounds and depends on nothing outside the formulas themselves.

There is a closed form. Substituting and solving gives price = fixed cost / (1 − margin − partner share), and this page computes it alongside the iteration to confirm they agree. That algebra is obvious on a page and genuinely awkward to express across spreadsheet cells that each hold one step of a business calculation people need to read and audit. The iteration was the right call in the sheet; the closed form is what you'd write in code.

The closed form also exposes something the twenty unrolled blocks hide: if the partner share is greater than 1 − margin, the denominator goes negative and no price satisfies the target at all. Push the partner share above your margin headroom on the sliders and the model says so instead of quietly producing a number.
