Job Search Ops Platform

I got laid off in August 2026, and instead of grinding through applications I spent the first week building the system I actually needed. It runs my search now: four linked Notion databases, a verified bank of my real accomplishments, and three scheduled automations doing the repetitive parts.

The problem

The same week I was laid off, my LinkedIn inbox filled up with recruiter messages, job alerts, and warm intros. Within days I had lost track of who I had talked to, which job descriptions I had actually read closely, and which version of my resume went where. A spreadsheet was not going to hold up once volume picked up, and I had been burned before by generic untailored applications going nowhere.

Four databases, not one table

  • Applications. Every role I am tracking, including ones I have not applied to yet. A Staleness formula flags anything sitting without movement for 3+ days, so nothing quietly dies from neglect.
  • LinkedIn Outreach. Every person from comments, DMs, and InMails, with its own connection-status pipeline and its own staleness tracking.
  • Email Sync Log. A running record of what Gmail is telling me, cross-referenced against the applications it relates to.
  • Meeting Prep & Notes. Relation-linked to Applications, populated with company research and interviewer background before every call.

Alongside those sits a Mission Statement page holding my salary floor, location preference, and seniority target. Every automated priority score gets weighed against it, so the system ranks against what I actually want instead of against whatever arrived most recently.

The hardest problem was accuracy, not automation

Early on I fed Claude a pile of source material, old resumes and cover letters and a LinkedIn export, and asked it to build a bank of my real accomplishments for the tailoring pipeline to pull from. It found two problems immediately.

One cover letter described me tracing a production bug through raw JSON metadata and building a 23-scenario test matrix. It was detailed enough that it read as suspicious rather than impressive. A resume draft claimed a platform migration was fully "modernized," but the actual project doc from my last day showed it still sitting in sandbox with three open blockers.

Neither was intentional dishonesty. It is what happens when you draft application materials over months with AI help, and the story drifts a little each time it gets rewritten. It is also exactly the kind of thing that falls apart under a real follow-up question in an interview.

So the Background Bank became a verification layer instead of a content store. Every claim traces to a real source. Conflicting numbers across documents get flagged rather than silently resolved. Anything unconfirmed stays out until I sign off on it. Every automated resume and cover letter pulls only from that page. That is the difference between AI helping me apply faster and AI helping me apply faster while also helping me lie.

What the automation actually does

Three scheduled skills, each with one narrow job.

  • job-application-tailor reads a pasted job description, cross-references it against the Background Bank, and produces an ATS-safe tailored resume, a matching cover letter, and a spoken "tell me about yourself" script. Where a requirement isn't supported by my real background, it flags the gap instead of inventing something to fill it.
  • meeting-investigator runs before scheduled calls: company and product research, an honest green-flag and red-flag read, and short professional profiles of who I am meeting, scoped strictly to public professional sources.
  • linkedin-alert-parser processes LinkedIn's own job-alert emails and dedupes against what is already tracked.

Two bugs worth describing

The first version of the alert parser deduped on the job board's tracking ID. That turned out to be unreliable, because the same underlying posting can be issued several different IDs across reposts, and I nearly applied twice to one role. The fix was to dedupe on the semantic content instead, company plus role, fuzzy-matched, and treat the ID as a secondary signal rather than the primary one. I also added a check for listings coming through a staffing agency or job board reposting someone else's opening under its own name.

The second showed up in the weekday morning digest, which runs on a schedule rather than a trigger. It diffs the tracker against its own prior state instead of dumping current data, and cross-checks it against my real calendar. That cross-check is where the value turned out to be. It once caught a meeting-prep note saying one time while the real calendar invite, updated later, said another, and the new time landed exactly on top of a separate confirmed interview. Neither the tracker nor the previous digest had noticed. Making that reliable meant two changes: trust the calendar event's actual timestamp over any note written before a reschedule, and diff against the digest's own last-updated date instead of a hardcoded day count, so a Monday run rolls up the whole weekend instead of assuming nothing happened since Friday.

Notion API notes, for anyone building something similar

  • Formula DDL needs the expression wrapped as a single-quoted string literal. Bare syntax throws a cryptic "unexpected character" error that looks like an operator problem and isn't.
  • Emoji embedded directly in a formula's string output can silently corrupt on write. Plain text labels coloured through Notion's native select and status types are more reliable as a status indicator.
  • A SQL aggregate query can report a genuinely empty field as 100% filled if a case-insensitively similar field name exists elsewhere in the schema. Verify with a direct record fetch before trusting an aggregate.

Giving it away

Once it was actually working I stripped out everything personal and packaged the whole thing as a deployable kit. The bootstrap prompt walks a fresh Claude and Notion setup through interviewing someone about their own background, building their own verified Background Bank, standing up the same four-database architecture, and installing genericized versions of the three skills.

Three extra pieces went in along the way. A checklist for catching AI-sounding writing before it reaches a resume, which opens on em-dash overuse and which I have since had to apply to this very site. A short guide on where the default tech-style resume format is the wrong call, because academia, government, legal, and creative fields all have real and different conventions. And the digest automation itself, with one deliberate change: my version closes each email with a quote from a personal pool of sources, and the generalized version does not carry that pool forward. It asks the next person what they would want, or whether they want a quote at all.

Not inventing facts about someone's career and not quietly imposing your own taste on someone else's tool turned out to be the same discipline applied twice.

Where it stands

The tracker holds over 160 opportunities across LinkedIn alerts, referrals, and direct recruiter outreach, with tailoring and pre-meeting research running against real scheduled interviews. So far it has caught a same-role duplicate before it became a second application, disambiguated two different people both named Tom in my own notes, flagged a factual overclaim in my own resume draft before it reached a recruiter, and found a real double-booking between two confirmed interview rounds a few days before either would have happened.

None of that replaces interviewing well or following up on relationships. The highest-signal outcomes in this search have consistently come from direct networking conversations, not from volume. What the system does is clear the busywork around that, so there is more time for the part that actually moves things forward.
