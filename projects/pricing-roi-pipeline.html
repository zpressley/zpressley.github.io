Pricing-to-ROI Pipeline

Pricing had a circular problem nobody had solved cleanly. The price of a deal depended on its cost, and one of the cost lines was defined as a percentage of the final price. Before this, the CFO priced every deal by hand: guess a price, check the margin, adjust, repeat.

A price that depends on a cost that depends on the price

Price comes from cost and a target margin: price = cost / (1 − margin). But one cost line was itself a percentage of the final price, and another scaled with projected usage that also moved with price. Neither side resolves until the other is already known, and there's no single-pass formula for that.

Why not just turn on iterative calculation

Google Sheets has a setting for exactly this. I didn't use it, for one reason: it's a file-level toggle. If a collaborator works in a copy where it's switched off, the model doesn't error. It silently returns the wrong number. A pricing tool whose correctness depends on a checkbox in somebody else's settings menu is worse than one that doesn't run at all, because you can't tell by looking.

So I unrolled the loop by hand into roughly twenty sequential blocks: compute cost from the prior price, compute price from that cost, repeat. Each round nudges the estimate closer, and because the circular line is a small fraction of the total, the correction shrinks every round. It converges within a handful of iterations. The rest of the chain is margin for safety, not because anything is still moving. An input goes in, a converged price comes out, and it depends on nothing but the formulas themselves.

The demo runs that model live: move the inputs and watch the rounds settle, or push the percentage cost past your margin headroom and watch the whole thing stop having an answer.

Tiered volume pricing, capped at every boundary

A separate part of the engine handled volume tiers. Cost stepped down as volume rose, with an overage rate applied above each tier's ceiling. I capped that overage with MIN() against the next tier's flat price at every step, so usage just past a boundary can never cost more via overage than simply being moved up a tier would.

Without that cap you get an invoice where buying more costs less than buying slightly less, which is the kind of thing a customer finds once and never forgets. Nine or ten tiers deep, with nested conditionals doing tier lookup on top of the cap.

The client-facing side

On top of the engine sat a calculator a rep could run with a prospect's own numbers: loan volume, conversion rate, average deal size. It computed monthly volume, monthly revenue, and total cost under both the legacy platform and the new one, then derived an ROI multiple from the delta.

I colour-separated the inputs by source: one colour for model assumptions the company set, another for the client's own figures. A rep could see at a glance which numbers were theirs to change and which weren't. That matters when you're sitting across from someone asking where a number came from.

And then it wrote itself up

The output document was merge-coded against the calculator's output row, so a finished calculation became a formatted client-ready one-pager without anyone copying figures between files. Every hand-copied number is a chance to paste the wrong one into a document with a customer's name on it.

What I'd keep and what I'd change

The unrolled iteration was the right call in a spreadsheet, and I'd make it again there. In code it's unnecessary. Substituting the circular term and solving gives a closed form that lands on the same number in one step, and the demo computes both to show they agree. The algebra is trivial on paper and awkward across spreadsheet cells that each have to hold one readable, auditable step of a business calculation.

The closed form does surface one thing twenty unrolled blocks hide. When the percentage cost exceeds the margin headroom, there is no price that satisfies the target. Not a large price, none. The iteration just runs away, and a chain of blocks doesn't announce that. However you build it, that's an edge case worth making loud instead of letting someone discover it in front of a customer.
